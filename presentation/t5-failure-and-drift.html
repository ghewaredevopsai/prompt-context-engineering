Failure, drift,
and a repeatable check

Four ways an answer goes wrong, each with its own tell. Three ways a prompt that worked in March stops working in June. And the only thing that catches any of it: something you wrote down before you needed it.

4 slides · ~8 minutes Lab 5 follows · break it, repair it, lock it Trainer: Rajesh Gheware

The taxonomy

Four failures, four different tells

Only the top-right one is dangerous at scale. The other three announce themselves. Plausible wrong code is the failure that survives review, ships, and is found by a customer — which is why the counter for it is never "read more carefully".

Ask the room which quadrant they have actually been bitten by. It is almost always top-right, and almost always in something numeric: money, dates, boundaries. That is not a coincidence - those are the places where wrong code and right code look identical.

Drift

A prompt is not a program. It rots from three directions at once.

Green tests are not evidence here. If the model wrote both the code and the test, a passing suite tells you they agree with each other. Your five checks have to come from the rules, not from the answer.

The middle arm is the one teams are least prepared for: nobody diffs a vendor's model update, and a prompt tuned tightly to one model's habits is the most fragile thing in the repository. This is the argument for checks that assert on the rule rather than on the exact wording of the output.

The two you cannot write

are the two you were relying on a person to judge, every time, forever.

Either turn them into something checkable, or accept out loud that this prompt needs a human in the loop — and say which human.

Lab 5 now: break your Lab 1 prompt three prescribed ways, repair it, then write five rows. Record violations before, broken and repaired — and how many of your own five checks a fresh run passes.

Discovering that three of five are unwritable is the intended outcome, not a failure of the exercise. Say so before they start or half the room will think they did it wrong. The useful move is to keep pushing on one of the vague rows until it becomes checkable - "idiomatic" usually decomposes into two or three specific house rules that are.
